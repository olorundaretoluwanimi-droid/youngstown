# What Happened to Youngstown? The Day the Steel Mills Closed — script


## The Charge (cold open)

**h01** On Monday, 19 September 1977, the union presidents at Youngstown Sheet and Tube were told to come to a meeting at ten in the morning.
**h02** A company manager handed them a statement and read it aloud. Most of the Campbell Works was closing. About 5,000 people would lose their jobs.
**h03** One of the union men interrupted him with a question.
**w01** WITNESS CARD (silent): “Are you cutting the cord? Are they done?” “Yes.” — Ed Mann · and the company’s answer, PRESIDENT, LOCAL 1462 · 19 SEPTEMBER 1977
**h04** Youngstown, Ohio. In 1930, 170,002 people lived here. In 2020, 60,068.
**h05** Two years after that Monday, when a second steel company said it was leaving too, the steelworkers of this town did something almost no town had tried.
**h06** They took the company to federal court. And they asked a judge to rule that a town has a right to the mill it built its life around.
**h07** Tonight, you'll hear that case. The charge. The evidence. The witnesses. The company's defence. And the verdict. Then you decide.
**h08** This is for anyone who ever packed a lunch for a shift like that. This is Mill Town.

## Part 1 · Opening Statement

**a01** Part one. The opening statement.
**a02** The Mahoning River runs south-east out of Warren, through Niles and Girard, through Youngstown, Campbell and Struthers, and on into Pennsylvania.
**a03** People were making iron along this river by the early 1800s. There was coal in the hills, and limestone, and water.
**a04** In 1900, a group of Youngstown men raised $600,000 and started a company of their own. They called it the Youngstown Iron Sheet and Tube Company.
**a05** It was a local company, run by local families. It would grow into one of the biggest steelmakers in America.
**a06** In 1901, the Ohio Works, on the west side of town, began making steel for a new giant: the United States Steel Corporation.
**a07** In 1918, U.S. Steel added the McDonald Works, a few miles upriver.
**a08** And on 20 September that year, at the Brier Hill Works, a girl named Mary Jeannette Thomas lit a brand-new blast furnace.
**a09** They named it after her. Jeannette. Everyone in town called it Jenny.
**a10** Four great mills along one river, and more besides. And around them, a city grew.
**a11** By 1930, Youngstown had 170,002 people. Many had crossed an ocean to work in steel.
**a12** So here is the first thing the jury should ask. What did the mills give this town? And what did the town give back?

## Part 2 · Exhibit A: What the Town Built

**b01** Part two. Exhibit A. What the town built.
**b02** In 1944, the government sent a film crew to Youngstown to show the country how steel was made for the war.
**b03** They called the film Steel Town. These are the people they found.
**b04** Italians on Brier Hill. Slovaks, Hungarians, Greeks, Poles, and many more. And Black families who had come north from the South.
**b05** The work was hot, loud and dangerous. Men stood a few feet from molten iron for eight hours at a time.
**b06** They fought for a union, and in the summer of 1937, people were killed in Youngstown in that fight. But the union came.
**b07** And with it, a mill job that paid for a house. A car. A summer week at the lake. Children who went to college.
**b08** By 1950, Sheet and Tube alone employed about 27,000 people.
**b09** Years later, one of those steelworkers described the job in a single sentence.
**w02** WITNESS CARD (silent): “Every day you put your life on the line when you went into that iron house.” — Ed Mann, BRIER HILL STEELWORKER · SPEAKING IN 1980
**b10** This was the bargain. The town gave the mills its sons, its daughters, its air and its river. The mills gave the town a living.
**b11** But a bargain needs two sides. So who owned all this?

## Part 3 · Exhibit B: The Merger

**c01** Part three. Exhibit B. The merger.
**c02** In 1969, Youngstown Sheet and Tube merged with the Lykes Corporation, a shipping company from New Orleans.
**c03** The decisions about Youngstown's biggest employer were now made a thousand miles away, by a company that did not make steel.
**c04** The merger left Sheet and Tube carrying heavy debt. And the money that was spent on steel went mostly to the company's newer works near Chicago.
**c05** Meanwhile, the Campbell Works was getting old. Cheaper steel was arriving from Japan and Europe.
**c06** In the summer of 1977, the union men asked the managers whether the plant was in trouble. Under oath, later, they said the answer was: “We'll make it.”
**c07** Then came Monday, 19 September.
**c08** The statement said the company would move a major part of its steelmaking to Indiana Harbor, near Chicago. The cutback would require the lay-off or termination of approximately 5,000 employees.
**c09** There had been no warning. There was no law that said there had to be.
**c10** Youngstown still has a name for that day. Black Monday.
**c11** At Thanksgiving, 185 clergy from across the valley signed a pastoral letter. It opened with one line.
**w03** WITNESS CARD (silent): “Our community was wounded on September 19.” — Pastoral letter, 185 RELIGIOUS LEADERS OF THE MAHONING VALLEY · NOV 1977
**c12** And then the people who signed it asked a question nobody had asked before. Could the town buy its own mill back?

## Part 4 · The Witnesses

**d01** Part four. The witnesses.
**d02** The first to come forward were churchmen. The Catholic bishop of Youngstown, James Malone. The Episcopal bishop of Ohio, John Burt.
**d03** They formed the Ecumenical Coalition of the Mahoning Valley, and they set out to reopen the Campbell Works, owned by its workers and its community.
**d04** It would cost hundreds of millions of dollars. So they asked the town to show Washington that it meant it.
**d05** In February 1978, at a church service in Boardman, they opened the Save Our Valley campaign. People opened savings accounts that could one day become shares in the mill.
**d06** By that November, there were 4,138 accounts. Just over $4 million.
**d07** It was never going to be enough on its own. The plan needed federal loan guarantees. And for months, the town waited for Washington.
**d08** On 29 March 1979, a steelworker named Len Balluck stood in front of the reporters.
**w04** WITNESS CARD (silent): “They threw 5,000 lives on a human garbage pile.” — Len Balluck, CAMPBELL WORKS STEELWORKER FOR 20 YEARS · 29 MARCH 1979
**d09** The next morning, a letter arrived from the Commerce Department. The plan needed $245 million in guarantees. The limit for any one company, the letter said, was $100 million.
**d10** The answer was no.
**d11** The national steelworkers' union, in Pittsburgh, had its own strategy for the whole industry, and it kept its distance from the fight in Youngstown.
**d12** And by then, the second wave had already come. Sheet and Tube had been merged again, into a bigger company, and the Brier Hill Works was to close by the end of 1979.
**d13** About 1,500 more jobs. By the end of the year, Brier Hill was silent.
**d14** That left one company in town still making steel. U.S. Steel. Was anyone still promising to stay?

## Part 5 · Exhibit C: The Promise

**e01** Part five. Exhibit C. The promise.
**e02** U.S. Steel's Ohio and McDonald Works employed about 3,500 people. And they could hear the news from down the river.
**e03** The company had a telephone hot line its workers could call for news. Every message was recorded. Later, they would be read out in court.
**e04** On 1 September 1977, 18 days before Black Monday, the superintendent, Bill Ashton, recorded this: “There are no immediate plans to permanently shut down either the Ohio Works or McDonald Mills.”
**e05** But he added: “The continued operation of these plants is absolutely dependent upon their being profit-makers.”
**e06** So the workers set out to make them profitable. In February 1978, a new superintendent took over. His name was Bill Kirwan.
**e07** That April, he had good news for the hot line. “In the month of March, for the first time in a long, long time, the Youngstown Works earned a profit.”
**e08** And he signed off like this: “Keep up the momentum — our future is what we make it.”
**e09** At the end of the year, he thanked every man and woman at the two mills. The goal for 1978, he said, had been “survival.” The goal for 1979 was “revival.”
**e10** In June 1979, U.S. Steel's chairman, David Roderick, said it on television: “Simply stated, we have no plans for shutting down our Youngstown operation.”
**e11** On 27 November 1979, a steelworker named Frank Georges bought a new house. Driving home from the bank, he heard the news on his car radio.
**e12** U.S. Steel was closing the Ohio Works and the McDonald Works. 3,500 more jobs.
**e13** On a freezing Monday in January 1980, the steelworkers met at the Local 1330 union hall. U.S. Steel's district headquarters was just down the hill.
**e14** Ed Mann, whose own mill at Brier Hill was already gone, stood up and told the meeting where he was going.
**w05** WITNESS CARD (silent): “Now, I’m going down that hill and I’m going into that building.” — Ed Mann, PRESIDENT, LOCAL 1462 · 28 JANUARY 1980
**e15** Hundreds followed him. The door gave way. For several hours, the steelworkers held the building, and a banner hung from the upper windows: “Keep Our Mills Open.”
**e16** They had believed a promise. But can a promise like that hold up in court?

## Part 6 · The Trial

**f01** Part six. The trial.
**f02** On 21 December 1979, the steelworkers had filed suit. Local 1330, United Steel Workers of America, against the United States Steel Corporation.
**f03** Their lawyer was Staughton Lynd, a historian turned labour lawyer. The case went to a federal judge in Cleveland, Thomas Lambros.
**f04** The workers made two arguments. First: the company had made a promise, and they had kept their side of it.
**f05** And second, an idea no American court had ever accepted. That after eighty years, the town itself had a claim on the mills.
**f06** In February, U.S. Steel told the court it would shut the mills sooner than planned. Judge Lambros called both sides in.
**f07** And on 28 February 1980, the judge himself said something nobody in the room expected.
**f08** “Everything that has happened in the Mahoning Valley has been happening for many years because of steel. Schools have been built, roads have been built.”
**f09** “It seems to me that a property right has arisen from this lengthy, long-established relationship.”
**f10** He ordered U.S. Steel to keep the mills open until the case was heard. And he moved the trial to Youngstown, close enough to Pittsburgh that the company's chairman could be made to testify.
**f11** The trial opened on 17 March 1980. It lasted five days.
**f12** The workers had made their case. Now, what would the company say for itself?

## Part 7 · The Defence

**g01** Part seven. The defence.
**g02** U.S. Steel's answer was simple. The mills were losing money, and nothing the workers did could change that.
**g03** The company's president testified that the main cause was obsolescence. The Ohio Works still made its steel in open-hearth furnaces, a method most of the industry was leaving behind.
**g04** Modern mills were moving to basic oxygen furnaces, electric furnaces, and continuous casters. Rebuilding Youngstown that way would cost hundreds of millions of dollars.
**g05** The chairman, David Roderick, was asked whether the Works had been about breaking even in 1979. “The actual number was about a $300 thousand loss, as of the end of October, cumulative for the year.”
**g06** The workers pointed to the company's own figures. Count only the day-to-day costs of making steel, and the Works came out more than $32 million ahead for 1980.
**g07** But count everything — the equipment wearing out, the selling costs, the overheads — and the company's own projection for 1980 was a loss of $9.4 million.
**g08** Same mills. Same year. Two honest ways of reading the books.
**g09** And the promise? Read the hot line again, the company said. Every message tied the future to profit. “If and when there will be a phase-out depends on the plant's profitability.”
**g10** Encouragement to a workforce, the company argued, is not a contract. And no law in Ohio or in America said a company could not close its own plant.
**g11** The workers had one more hope. Let us buy the mills, they said. Roderick testified U.S. Steel would not sell to a competitor backed by federal money.
**g12** Both sides have spoken. Who's right?

## Part 8 · The Verdict

**h11** Part eight. The verdict.
**h12** On the morning of Friday, 21 March 1980, the lawyers gave their closing arguments. Less than two hours later, Judge Lambros read his decision.
**h13** On the promise, he found that the company had never promised to keep the mills open if the workers made them profitable. And that they had not been profitable.
**h14** On the town's right to its mill, he wrote: “The mechanism … to recognize this new property right is not now in existence in the code of laws of our nation.”
**h15** “This Court is not a legislative body and can not make laws where none exist. … This Court can determine no legal basis for the finding of a property right.”
**h16** The workers appealed. In July, the appeals court in Cincinnati agreed with the judge, and wrote why.
**h17** “The former mill towns of New England, with their empty textile factory buildings, are monuments to the migration of textile manufacturers to the South.”
**h18** Deciding what should happen when a plant closes, the court said, is “the responsibility of the legislatures of the states or of the Congress of the United States.” Not of a judge.
**h19** The fires at the Ohio Works and the McDonald Works went out. In three years, Youngstown had lost about 10,000 steel jobs, and across the valley, tens of thousands more went with them.
**h20** Over the years that followed, the furnaces came down, one after another. Jenny was the last. She was torn down in January 1997.
**h21** The workers lost their case. But did it change anything?

## Part 9 · The Jury

**j01** Part nine. The jury.
**j02** Judge Lambros had written that bills about plant closings were already pending on Capitol Hill. It took eight more years.
**j03** In 1988, Congress passed the WARN Act. Most large employers must now give 60 days' notice before a plant closing or a mass layoff.
**j04** 60 days. On Black Monday, there had been none.
**j05** In 1992, the state opened a steel museum in Youngstown, in a building designed to look like a mill.
**j06** In 1995, Bruce Springsteen named a song after the town, and put Jenny in it.
**j07** And in 2012, a French steel company finished a $650 million mill for steel pipe in the Brier Hill district, close to where Jenny stood. It was built for about 350 jobs.
**j08** Youngstown today is a city of about 60,000. Fewer than in 1910.
**j09** So here is the case. A company that said its mills were losing money, and had every legal right to close them.
**j10** And a town that had given those mills eighty years, and believed that ought to count for something.
**j11** The law gave its answer in 1980. The question it could not answer is still here.
**j12** Does a town have any right to the mill it built its life around?
**j13** You've heard the case. What's your verdict?
**j14** Next time on Mill Town: Kenosha, Wisconsin.
